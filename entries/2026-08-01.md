**WORKFLOW — Claude Code creator Boris Cherny said the team deleted more than 80% of Claude Code's system prompt for Opus 5 using "ablation": remove an instruction, then test whether the result still holds.** *Source: The Neuron, "AI Skill of the Day," same July 31 issue.* The 5-step version for your own setup: (1) disable your existing system prompt, skills, hooks, and custom instructions; (2) give the model a real task with clear guardrails, exit criteria, and a way to verify the result; (3) watch what it handles correctly unaided; (4) add an instruction only after the model repeatedly makes the same mistake; (5) retest after every addition, since the model reads that instruction on every future task. Keep your evals, but retire them once newer models consistently pass.

**CONCEPT — A "rent vs. own" framework maps exactly which layers of an AI agent stack to keep portable so a vendor switch doesn't become a multi-month project.** *Source: thenuancedperspective (Chai and AI community, Substack), "Navigating Vendor Lock-in and Keeping Control of your Data," August 1.* The six layers: rent the model (swappable, but expect prompt re-tuning), own the interface (a gateway like LiteLLM turns a model swap into a config change), own your prompts/context (version them in a repo you control), own your data/embeddings (the heaviest lock-in — confirm you can export raw vectors, not just text), own your evaluations (download the eval set rather than relying on an observability vendor's UI), and rent orchestration (LangGraph/CrewAI — you won't switch often). Worked example: an insurance claims-triage agent built entirely on one vendor's platform hits a cheaper competing architecture two years in, but proving the switch is safe requires re-tuning prompts, re-embedding two years of data, and rebuilding evals from scratch — months of work before anyone can sign off.
